Backup power

Running it safely

Nevergenerators kill with carbon monoxide

  • Never run it in a garage, basement, shed or porch, even with the door open.
  • Never wire it in yourself or plug it into a wall outlet. Power fed back into the lines can kill the crew restoring your service.
  • Never refuel it hot. Let it cool first.
  • Never use thin household cords. They overheat.

Always

  • Run it outdoors, at least 20 feet from the house, with the exhaust pointed away from doors, windows and vents.
  • Store fuel outside the house.
  • Headache, dizziness or nausea? Get everyone outside, then call 911.

Extension cords

  • Use outdoor-rated cords with thick wire. The lower the gauge number, the thicker the wire.
  • 12-gauge for runs up to 50 feet. 10-gauge for 50 to 100 feet.
  • The heavy cord from a generator’s 30-amp outlet to a transfer switch is its own purchase. Buy the one made for that outlet.

Thinking of getting one?

  • Ask your electric utility first about home battery programs.
  • Hire a licensed electrician; your town’s emergency managers may know one.
  • Have the critical circuits moved to a subpanel: pumps, heat, refrigerator, medical equipment.
  • Size it from the table on page {{pg "gen3"}}, and decide where it will sit before you buy.

How much to store for two weeks

people ×gallons a day × 14 days =gallons

One gallon a person a day is the bare minimum, for drinking and a little cooking: 14 gallons each. Plan on 2 if you can. Washing up, a sick day and hard work all take more. Flushing and animals come on top of that (pages {{pg "plumbing"}} and {{pg "animals"}}).

The bathtub math Say the tub holds 50 gallons and four people live here.
4 people × 1 gallon = 4 gallons a day. 50 ÷ 4 = about 12 days
4 people × 2 gallons = 8 gallons a day. 50 ÷ 8 = about 6 days
Flush with it and it goes faster: a newer toilet uses about 1.6 gallons a flush. Keep tub water for washing and flushing, and drinking water in jugs.

What to stock

  • Food you already eat. Buy a little extra each trip and use the oldest first, so nothing goes to waste.
  • Meals that need no cooking, or only hot water: canned meat, fish, beans and fruit, peanut butter, crackers, oatmeal, cereal, shelf-stable milk.
  • Go easy on salty food. It makes you thirsty when water is short.
  • A manual can opener, and paper plates to save wash water.
  • Something that feels like a treat. Two weeks is a long time.
  • If rotating food is more than you want to manage, look at freeze-dried emergency meals sold with a 25-year shelf life. They need water, usually hot.

Safe to eat, safe to drink

What keeps, what to throw out, and how to make water safe

Food when the power is out

With the door kept shutIt stays cold about
Refrigerator4 hours
Freezer, full48 hours
Freezer, half full24 hours
Make it last Eat from the refrigerator first, then the freezer, then the shelf. Keep the doors shut, and tape them so nobody forgets. In winter, use the giant freezer outdoors: pack food in a cooler or tote outside, below 32°F, where animals can’t get at it. Frozen food that still has ice crystals in it is safe to cook or refreeze.
When in doubt, throw it out Throw out meat, eggs, dairy and leftovers that have been above 40°F for more than 2 hours. Never taste food to test it.
Thermometers:refrigerator, 40°F or beloweach freezer, 0°F

Water you can drink

Making water safe Bring it to a rolling boil for 1 minute. Or add 8 drops of plain unscented bleach to a gallon, stir, and wait 30 minutes. Treat water from a brook, a spring or the bathtub before you drink it. If floodwater reached your well, don’t drink from it until it has been tested.
Storing water Store-bought water keeps until the date on the bottle. Water you bottle yourself, in clean food-grade jugs, should be replaced every 6 months. Write the date on each jug and keep them somewhere cool and dark.

Carbon monoxide and fire

Staying warm

With the heat out, the cold itself is the danger.

Our warm room

If the heat is out, we will all live in

Seal off the other rooms with plastic sheeting, blankets and duct tape.

Smoke and CO alarms have battery backup.Tested
Too cold: know the signs

Hypothermia: shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, drowsiness. In a baby: bright red, cold skin and very low energy. A body temperature below 95°F is an emergency: get medical help right away. It can happen above 40°F when someone is wet and chilled.

Frostbite: numbness, and white or grayish-yellow skin that feels firm or waxy. The person may not feel it. Get to a warm room and warm the skin with body heat or warm water. No heating pad, stove or rubbing: numb skin burns easily.

First aid

When the roads are closed, help can be a long time coming.

Our first aid kit is keptrestocked on

What to keep

For everyday injuries

  • Disposable nitrile gloves, hand sanitizer
  • Gauze pads and rolls, bandages, medical tape
  • Elastic wraps, a moldable splint (SAM splint), a blister kit
  • Instant cold packs, and heat packs you boil to reset
  • Emergency blankets
  • Tweezers, shears, thermometer

For serious bleeding

  • A Stop the Bleed kit: tourniquet, packing gauze, pressure bandage, gloves, shears

Buy it from the Stop the Bleed program itself, at stopthebleed.org. Cheap copies sold online can fail when it counts.

From the pharmacy shelf

  • Pain and fever reducer, allergy medicine, antacid, anti-diarrheal, rehydration salts
Keep it simple, and keep it fresh
  • Stock what someone here knows how to use. Gear nobody has trained on does not help.
  • First aid supplies expire, even sterile gauze, in about 3 to 5 years. Check the dates each fall and replace what you use.

Classes worth taking

  • Stop the Bleed. How to stop life-threatening bleeding. Hands-on and often free. Find a class at stopthebleed.org.
  • First aid and CPR. The American Red Cross (redcross.org), or ask your rescue squad or fire department.
  • Wilderness first aid. For when help is hours away. SOLO in Conway, NH (soloschools.com) teaches it and sells a pocket Wilderness First Aid Map for its graduates. A free online course is at openwfa.com.

Gravity or pumped? A gravity system keeps working without power as long as you have water to flush with: pour a bucket quickly into the bowl. A pumped system does not. The pump chamber fills, and then it backs up into the house. Use as little water as you can, and find out how much reserve the chamber holds.
Worst case, use a bucket Two weeks of flushing takes more water than two weeks of drinking. If you can’t spare it, line a 5-gallon bucket with a heavy trash bag, add sawdust, wood ash or cat litter after each use, and keep the tied bag outside.

During the emergency

It may take days. Be patient. Be nice.

Do

  • Stay calm and stay safe. Move slower than usual and think each job through.
  • Drink water and eat well.
  • Lift and shovel the right way, and rest often.
  • Check on your neighbors. Help each other.
  • Turn off the water, gas or electricity if you need to (map inside the back cover).
  • If you can’t keep warm, leave early for a neighbor’s, a warming place or the shelter.
Do not make your own emergency

Fire trucks, ambulances and police may not be able to get to you, and help may not come in time. Do not:

  • Eat spoiled food
  • Drink bad water
  • Go near downed power lines
  • Run a generator indoors, or wire one in yourself
  • Heat the house with a gas stove or grill
  • Burn in a chimney that has not been inspected
  • Thaw pipes with a torch
  • Take chances with ladders, roof rakes or chainsaws
  • Overexert. Shoveling can bring on a heart attack.

Help your town

In Vermont the town usually runs the shelter, with the Red Cross lending a hand, and a small town runs on volunteers. The best time to offer is before the storm.

  • Tell your Emergency Management Director, fire department or road crew what you can do: check on neighbors, staff the shelter, cook, run a chainsaw or a tractor, lend a generator
  • Take first aid, CPR and Stop the Bleed classes (page {{pg "firstaid"}})

First steps, and what we learned

First

  • Take photos of the damage and the spoiled food before you clean up. Keep receipts.

  • Tell your town’s Emergency Management Director (inside the front cover) and call your insurance company.

An ice storm, a flood that takes out the road, a big snow: any of them can leave a house without power, or cut off from town, for days and sometimes for two weeks. Most advice plans for three days. This workbook plans for two weeks: help cannot reach everyone right away, and a household ready for two weeks can ride out almost anything shorter.

This is our best advice, not a rulebook. You know your house, your family and your needs best. Take what fits and change what doesn’t.

Ask three things about every system in the house
  1. What does it need to run?
  2. What happens when it stops?
  3. What is our backup, and can everyone here use it?
“One is none,
two is one.”
{{end}} {{define "kys-how"}}
  1. Walk the house together. Everyone who lives here, pencil in hand.
  2. Take it a page at a time. Getting prepared can feel like a lot, so go at your own pace. A blank line is just a question to chase down.
  3. Try every backup once. Start the generator. Find the shutoff and turn it.
  4. Picture day ten, not just the first night. What runs out first? Thinking it through now keeps you calmer then.
  5. Keep this workbook where everyone can find it: in your outage bucket, a kitchen drawer, or with the flashlights. Go through it again each fall.
